Almost every useful claim compares numbers: a balance covers a payment, a person is over 18, a
price is under a limit. A circuit can't compare numbers directly. This page explains why, and how a
**range proof** makes comparisons possible.

## A prime field has no order

A circuit computes over a [finite field](/docs/learn-zk/02-circuits-and-constraints): the integers
modulo a prime `p`, a 254-bit number for BN254. Addition wraps around at `p`, like a clock with `p`
hours.

On a clock, "less than" stops meaning anything. Start at 0 and keep adding 1, and every step
seems to go up: 0 < 1 < 2 < … until, after `p` steps, you're back at 0. An order that respects
addition (if `a < b` then `a + c < b + c`) would make 0 less than itself, so a prime field can't
have one. Any order you choose would be broken by the arithmetic.

The wrap is easy to see. In the field, `0 − 1` isn't −1; it's `p − 1`, the largest value there is.
So "is the balance at least the payment?" can't be asked of field elements as they stand: a
prover could put `p − 1` where a small negative number should be, and it would look enormous.

## Fix a range, and prove it

The way out is to give up on comparing *field elements* and compare *small integers* instead. If
`a` and `b` are known to lie in `[0, 2^N)`, with `2^N` far below `p`, then nothing can wrap. Field
arithmetic on them is integer arithmetic, and integer order means what it should.

The circuit can't take "known to lie in" on trust: the prover chooses the values. It has to prove
it. That proof is a **bit decomposition**: the prover supplies `N` bits `b₀ … b_{N−1}`, and the
circuit checks two things:

- each bit is a bit: `bᵢ · (bᵢ − 1) = 0`, which holds only for 0 and 1;
- the bits make the value: `a = b₀ + 2·b₁ + 4·b₂ + … + 2^{N−1}·b_{N−1}`.

`N` bits can only add up to something in `[0, 2^N)`, so a value with a valid decomposition is in
range. A value outside it, `p − 1` say, has no such bits, and no proof exists. That is a **range
proof**. It costs one constraint per bit.

## Comparing two numbers

With `a` and `b` both range-checked to `N` bits, `a < b` is one more decomposition. The circuit
takes `a − b + 2^N`. That's a number between 1 and `2^{N+1} − 1`, so it fits in `N + 1` bits and
can't wrap. Its top bit is 1 exactly when `a ≥ b`. The circuit decomposes it and reads that bit.

Switch the range checks off and try `b = -1`: the comparison then reports something false. Range
checks are what make a comparison mean anything.

## What this means in xark

You rarely write a decomposition yourself; the types do it.

- **Integer inputs are range-checked as they're read.** A `u64` input costs 64 constraints, a `u8`
  costs 8, a `Days<1900, 2100>` costs 17. That check happens once per value, however many times you
  compare it.
- **A comparison adds about one more decomposition**, of the difference.
  [Over 9000](/docs/tutorials/01-over-9000)'s whole circuit is 131 constraints: 64 for
  `power_level`, 64 for the difference the comparison splits, two for the comparison itself, and one
  equality.
- **A bare `Field` has no `<`.** Comparing two `Field`s needs a width you state: `a.lt::<32>(b)`.
  Comparing against a typed literal takes the literal's width: `x < 100u32`. There is no silent
  default. See [The xark crate](/docs/reference/language#field).
- **Sums can't wrap either.** When every amount is a range-checked `u64`, a sum of a few of them
  stays far below `p`. So `inputs == outputs` means what it says, with no "negative" note minting
  money. [Shielded transfer](/docs/tutorials/05-shielded-transfer) relies on exactly this.
